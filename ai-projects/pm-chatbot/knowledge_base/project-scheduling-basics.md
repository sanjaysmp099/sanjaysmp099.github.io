title: Project scheduling fundamentals

Building a project schedule generally starts with defining the work through
a Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) — decomposing the overall project into
smaller, manageable pieces down to individual tasks or work packages.

Once tasks are defined, dependencies between them are mapped. A
finish-to-start dependency, the most common type, means one task cannot
begin until another finishes. Other dependency types (start-to-start,
finish-to-finish, start-to-finish) exist but are used less frequently.

The Critical Path Method (CPM) is a widely used technique for scheduling.
It identifies the longest sequence of dependent tasks through the project —
the "critical path" — which determines the shortest possible project
duration. Any delay to a task on the critical path delays the entire
project, while tasks not on the critical path have some scheduling
flexibility, known as float or slack.

Estimating task duration is often the hardest part of scheduling. Common
approaches include analogous estimating (comparing to similar past tasks),
parametric estimating (using a rate, like hours per unit of work), and
three-point estimating, which combines optimistic, pessimistic, and most
likely estimates into a weighted average to account for uncertainty.

Once a schedule is built, it's rarely static — it should be updated
regularly as actual progress is compared against the plan, and re-baselined
when scope or timeline changes are formally approved.
